# Writing Voice Guide

## How to use this
The first pass at this guide just mirrored back the rules you stated. This version adds the patterns that show up *in the writing itself* but that you didn't name outright — the structural habits underneath the sentence-level rules. Explicit rules and extrapolated ones are labeled separately so you can tell which is which.

---

## 1. Explicit Rules (things you stated directly)

**Mechanics**
- No em-dashes.
- No mid-sentence fragments — restructure around "which," "that," "because," rather than breaking with a dash or comma.
- Max two adjectives before a noun; restructure rather than stack more.
- Oxford commas, always.
- Semicolons are rare.
- Max ~2 "ands" per sentence, often fewer; comfortable with "but," "so," "yet" but without piling up.
- "i.e.," to restate/clarify; "e.g.," for a specific example or use case.
- "However" starts a new sentence, after a period, followed by a comma.
- "For instance" / "for example" used occasionally, not reflexively.

**Word choice**
- Avoid jargon; "use" not "utilize."
- Spell out acronyms on first use.
- "I believe" over "I think."
- Direct assertions preferred over hedges like "might" or "it seems" — though see Section 2 on how hedging actually shows up.

**Register**
- Formal: no contractions, first person plural ("we tested," "we found"), fully rule-compliant.
- Casual: contractions, jokes, rhetorical questions allowed. Same core sentence discipline still applies but delivery loosens.
- Front-loaded topic sentences in both registers.
- Bullet points/lists fine in casual or applied writing; full prose preferred in academic writing.

## 2. Extrapolated Patterns (from the two writing samples)

**"Can" is your real hedge word, not "might."**
Both samples lean on "can" constantly to express possibility or capacity — "can be rich in both native and planted species," "can help mitigate," "can quickly accumulate," "can more effectively inform." This isn't uncertainty-hedging, it's capacity-hedging: it says a thing is *possible under the right conditions* without weakening the claim the way "might" or "perhaps" would. It reads as confident because "can" implies you know the mechanism, even though it isn't asserting the thing always happens. Worth naming explicitly since your stated aversion to hedging language is really an aversion to *epistemic* hedging, not *conditional* hedging.

**Cohesion by repetition, not pronoun substitution.**
Across both samples, key nouns get repeated ("GI type," "GI," "biodiversity," "P. smintheus") rather than replaced with "it" or "this." This trades a little elegance for zero ambiguity — a reader never has to backtrack to figure out what "it" refers to. This is a big driver of why the prose reads as precise rather than literary.

**Paragraphs have an hourglass shape.**
Both excerpts follow the same shape: broad claim, narrows into a specific mechanism or study detail, a concrete example or citation anchors it, then the final sentence broadens back out to significance or implication. This is a stronger structural signature than "topic sentence + conclusion sentence" alone, since it is specifically a narrow-then-open shape rather than just bookend sentences.

**Concession-then-rebuttal as a default move for complicating a claim.**
"Although there can be negative impacts of roadways... these effects can be mitigated" and "Despite standardized protocols, there can be variability..." concede a complication with "although" or "despite," then pivot to the main point in the same sentence. This is a compact way of acknowledging counterevidence without derailing the paragraph's argument, and it shows up instead of hedging the original claim itself.

**Adverbial sentence-openers as signposting.**
"Interestingly," "Additionally," "Similarly," "For instance" are single-word or short-phrase adverbial openers followed by a comma, and they are the primary transition device, more than subordinating clauses. It is a light touch: one word does the signaling, then the sentence proceeds normally.

**"Such as" does most of the example-work; "i.e./e.g." are reserved for tighter, more technical clarifications.**
"Such as" introduces broader illustrative examples ("such as increased collisions with vehicles"), while "i.e." and "e.g." are saved for more precise, parenthetical restatements. This is a distinction you did not name but consistently apply: "such as" for narrative examples, "i.e./e.g." for definitional ones.

**Findings are stated as comparatives, not standalone facts.**
"P. smintheus gained distributional area... but these gains were significantly reduced with the inclusion of Sedum sp." shows that results are almost always expressed relative to a baseline or comparison condition rather than as an isolated number or outcome. This tracks with a scientific-argument style, where a finding matters because of what it is different from.

## 3. Casual-Register Signals (pulled from your own messages, not the formal samples)

Looking back at how you've actually written to me in quick, unedited exchanges:
- Lowercase "i" and looser punctuation when writing fast, and apostrophes get dropped ("dont," "i dont want to schedule a meeting yet").
- Comma splices and run-on instructions are fine in this register, which is the opposite of the formal no-fragments rule. Casual writing prioritizes speed over structural precision.
- Even in fast, informal notes, "e.g." still shows up as a reflex. This is one of the few formal habits that survives into casual writing unchanged.
- Instructions to collaborators or AI tend to be terse and imperative, stacking short clauses with "and" rather than restructuring ("We typically require a minimum of $500, billed against that hourly at an average rate of ~$135, with...").

This suggests the formal and casual split is not total. A handful of habits, like e.g./i.e. precision and front-loading the ask, hold in both registers, while sentence discipline around fragments and adjective stacking is specifically a formal-writing constraint that loosens in quick, practical writing.
